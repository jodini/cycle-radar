# Cycle Radar Framework

## Why this exists

Markets repeatedly create opportunities because demand, inventories, capacity, credit and expectations adjust with delays. The objective is **not** to predict exact economic turning points. The objective is to identify industries where the direction of the next cycle is becoming asymmetric, then buy financially survivable businesses **before** that recovery is fully reflected in price.

Cheap does not automatically mean cyclical opportunity. We want:

**depressed economics + identifiable reversal mechanism + approaching normalization + survivable company + attractive valuation**

## Anatomy of a cycle

**D — Downturn**  
Demand, orders, pricing and margins deteriorate. Earnings estimates are still falling.

**T — Trough**  
Inventories and capacity adjust. Deterioration slows. Sentiment is usually still poor.

**R — Recovery**  
Orders, utilization and pricing begin to improve before reported earnings look good.

**E — Expansion**  
Volumes, margins and earnings compound. Operating leverage works in the investor's favor.

**L — Late cycle**  
Capacity investment returns, inventories rebuild and expectations become optimistic.

**P — Peak**  
Economics and reported results often look excellent, but forward returns deteriorate.

Then the cycle rolls back into **D**.

## How the stock price follows

Equity markets discount the future. Cyclical stocks commonly **bottom before earnings bottom** and **peak before earnings peak**.

A simplified sequence is:

- Early trough: valuation is cheap, but deterioration may still be continuing.
- **Late trough: Optimal Buy (OB)** — deterioration has largely stopped, yet consensus is still pessimistic.
- Early recovery: Buy / finish the position.
- Expansion: Hold.
- Late expansion: Trim.
- **Early peak: Optimal Sell (OS)** — reported results may still be excellent.
- Late peak / downturn: Sell or stay out.

The best-looking financial statements are often closer to the sell point than the buy point.

## Shock is an overlay, not a stage

A shock is an external disturbance that can hit any stage: war, embargo, pandemic, policy change, mine closure, route disruption or financial accident.

- **Negative shock near a trough** can create extraordinary entry prices, but survival must be checked first.
- **Positive shock in expansion/late cycle** can create temporary peak economics and should often be harvested rather than chased.
- When the shock fades, the underlying cycle usually reasserts itself.

A normal inventory glut, capacity build or destocking process is part of the cycle itself and should not be labelled as a shock.

## Action qualifiers

- **W — Watch:** learn the sector; too early or unclear.
- **B — Buy:** attractive, but not the best risk/reward point.
- **OB — Optimal Buy:** late trough to very early recovery.
- **H — Hold:** let the earnings recovery and operating leverage work.
- **T — Trim:** late expansion; valuation or expectations are becoming stretched.
- **OS — Optimal Sell:** early peak; the market is discounting peak economics.
- **S — Sell:** exit the remaining cyclical exposure.

## What can make the framework fail

A supposed cycle can actually be structural decline. Common failure modes:

- structural demand destruction;
- technological displacement;
- permanent excess capacity;
- geopolitical or regulatory regime change;
- balance-sheet failure before recovery;
- industry economics permanently resetting lower.

Every cycle must therefore track:

1. What confirms the cycle thesis?
2. What invalidates it?
3. Which indicators lead the turn?
4. Is the stock already pricing the next phase?
5. Can the company survive if the trough lasts longer than expected?

## Resolution and confidence

The canonical resolution is **quarterly**. Monthly data are useful only when a sector enters a suspected OB or OS window.

Quarter-level forecasts are most meaningful over the next 4–6 quarters. Beyond roughly two years, the calendar is a directional map, not precise timing. The entire radar should be recalibrated at least once per quarter.
